How to read the data: tap here
  • Height (elevation): the angle of a body above the horizon. 0° is right at the horizon; if negative, it is hidden below it.
  • Solar power: when the Sun is above the horizon, the panels generate power. The higher the % of light and the Sun height, the better.
  • Longest night: the longest continuous period without Sun. It shows how much battery or heat is needed to survive it.
  • Communication: if Earth is above the horizon there is a direct line, with no relay satellite. The % of time with Earth in view is shown.
  • Sets in / rises in: time left until the next change (set or rise). It is the operational countdown.
  • Sun and Earth together: the time with power and communication at once, when the mission can operate normally.
  • Horizon obstruction: simulates a hill or crater wall. Raise it a few degrees and see how much the result changes.
  • Verdict: a rough traffic light. Green if light and communication are good, amber if limited, red if difficult.
  • Height chart: the vertical axis is height in degrees (0° is the horizon) and the horizontal axis is the UTC date. The shaded areas under each curve are when the body is visible.
  • Windows (bars): three rows with the periods of Sun, Earth and both together. Empty gaps are night or loss of communication.
  • 3D view: the grey disc is the ground and the letters are compass points. Rotate by dragging. Earth rises and falls in waves because of the Moon’s libration.

Same site, same date. The only difference is a 2° obstruction on the horizon in scenario B. On the Shackleton rim the Sun never rises above 1.5° over a flat horizon, so that small obstacle is enough to leave the site without direct sunlight. That is why a few meters of location or one day of date can change an entire mission.

Limitations. The horizon is that of a spherical Moon plus a uniform obstruction angle that you choose (a terrain “mask”); real relief (LOLA) is not included. The calculation uses low-precision ephemerides (typical error of a few tenths of a degree), checked against the Apollo landings. Coordinates are approximate: verify them in Quickmap. Earth is drawn larger than the Sun because that is how it looks (1.9° versus 0.5°). Data and references. Moon and Sun positions are computed in this page with the series from Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms; no NASA dataset is loaded at run time. Site coordinates are approximate and should be checked against Quickmap (NASA/ASU, LRO). Use of AI. AI tools assisted with part of the code (formatting, comments and refactoring); this is declared in the project description and in the page metadata.
